## Rules for every type

- The title states the question's subject and the payoff. No clickbait, no year unless
  the content is genuinely tied to it.
- The opening answers the question in two or three sentences. Context comes after.
  A long article may give the answer as three to five short takeaways instead, each a
  full sentence a reader could act on.
- One `#` title, `##` for the sections a reader scans, `###` only when a section
  really has parts. Headings say what the section answers, and the follow-up
  questions from the brief appear in them in the reader's words.
- Each section can be quoted alone: it names its subject instead of saying "it" or
  "as mentioned above".
- Lists of criteria or options: one item per point, a short bold lead of two to four
  words, then one or two sentences. This is the one place bold leads belong.
- Numbers beat adjectives. "Free up to 2,500 executions a month (checked March 2026)"
  can be quoted; "generous free tier" cannot.
- The FAQ, when the article has one, is a `##` section with each question as a `###`
  heading, written the way a reader would ask it. The answer's first sentence answers;
  the whole answer stays within two or three sentences. Good questions are the
  difference between two options, the objection a buyer raises, and the long-tail
  variants of the main question. A question the article already answers in a section
  does not repeat there.
- Close with what to do next, not a summary of what was said.

## How-to

1. The result, and who it is for (opening).
2. What you need before starting.
3. Numbered steps, each with one action and how to check it worked. Commands and code
   in fenced blocks, copied from a run that worked.
4. Common errors and their fixes.
5. Next step (call to action fits here).

## Comparison

Use when the reader chooses between named options.

1. The verdict in short: which option for which reader.
2. How the options were judged: the criteria as a short list, and what the author used
   each option for. It comes before the options so the reader can weigh the verdicts.
3. A table: options as columns or rows, criteria the reader decides on. No criterion
   that every option shares. Use values where they exist (price, limits, hosting,
   licence, release year), each dated if it can change, and one "best for" row.
4. One section per option: a "Best for" line naming the reader it suits, what it is
   good at, where it falls short, who should skip it. Same order of points in every
   section.
5. A decision guide by situation: "if you need X, take A", grouped by the reader's
   size, industry, or constraint where those change the answer.

## Best options for a use case

A ranked list. Say how the list was chosen before the first entry, rank by fit for the
stated use case, and give each entry a one-line verdict, a "Best for" line, its
strengths, its limits, and who should skip it. Add the comparison table from the
Comparison type once the list has more than three entries. Name the best option even
when it pays nothing.

## Explainer

1. The definition in one or two sentences a newcomer understands.
2. Why it matters to this reader, with a concrete example.
3. How it works, in the depth the reader needs, no further.
4. When to use it and when not, and what to use instead.
5. Related terms, linked to the project's glossary if it has one.

## Cost guide

1. The realistic range, with what moves a project to each end.
2. The cost drivers, each with its effect.
3. Worked examples (from the author, marked as such).
4. Ways to lower the cost, and what that trades off.
5. Ongoing costs after launch.

Every price carries the date it was checked or comes from the author's own quotes.

## Checklist

Short context, then the checklist items, each with why it matters and how to check
it. Group items when there are more than about ten.
