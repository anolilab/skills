# Structure by article type

## Contents

- Rules for every type
- How-to
- Comparison
- Best options for a use case
- Explainer
- Cost guide
- Checklist

## Rules for every type

- The title states the question's subject and the payoff. No clickbait, no year unless
  the content is genuinely tied to it.
- The opening answers the question in two or three sentences. Context comes after.
- One `#` title, `##` for the sections a reader scans, `###` only when a section
  really has parts. Headings say what the section answers.
- Each section can be quoted alone: it names its subject instead of saying "it" or
  "as mentioned above".
- Close with what to do next, not a summary of what was said.

## How-to

1. The result, and who it is for (opening).
2. What you need before starting.
3. Numbered steps, each with one action and how to check it worked. Commands and code
   in fenced blocks, copied from a run that worked.
4. Common errors and their fixes.
5. Next step (call to action fits here).

## Comparison

Use when the reader chooses between named options.

1. The verdict in short: which option for which reader.
2. A table: options as columns or rows, criteria the reader decides on. No criterion
   that every option shares.
3. One section per option: what it is good at, where it falls short, who should pick
   it. Same order of points in every section.
4. How the options were judged (the method), and what the author used them for.
5. A decision guide: "if you need X, take A".

## Best options for a use case

A ranked list. Say how the list was chosen, rank by fit for the stated use case, and
give each entry a one-line verdict, its strengths, its limits, and who should skip it.
Name the best option even when it pays nothing.

## Explainer

1. The definition in one or two sentences a newcomer understands.
2. Why it matters to this reader, with a concrete example.
3. How it works, in the depth the reader needs, no further.
4. When to use it and when not, and what to use instead.
5. Related terms, linked to the project's glossary if it has one.

## Cost guide

1. The realistic range, with what moves a project to each end.
2. The cost drivers, each with its effect.
3. Worked examples (from the author, marked as such).
4. Ways to lower the cost, and what that trades off.
5. Ongoing costs after launch.

Every price carries the date it was checked or comes from the author's own quotes.

## Checklist

Short context, then the checklist items, each with why it matters and how to check
it. Group items when there are more than about ten.
