# Research

## What already answers the question

Search the reader's question as they would type it, in the article's language. Read
the top results and, where available, what AI answers say. For each, note:

- the answer it gives and how fast it gives it;
- what it gets wrong, leaves out, or hedges;
- whether it shows first-hand experience or only summarizes.

The gaps are the article's reason to exist. Write them into the brief.

## Sources

Prefer, in this order:

1. Primary sources: official documentation, pricing pages, release notes, laws and
   regulators, original studies with their method.
2. Reputable secondary sources that cite primary ones.
3. Practitioner reports (engineering blogs, conference talks) for experience, clearly
   framed as one team's experience.

Vendor blogs and listicles are leads to find the primary source, not sources
themselves. A statistic without a traceable origin does not go in.

## Verify before citing

Open every page you will cite and confirm the exact claim is on it, with the number
and its context. Record the date you checked. If two sources disagree, say so in the
article or pick the primary one and say why. If you cannot open a source, do not cite
it.

## Things that change

Prices, plan names, free tiers, limits, API versions, legal deadlines, and commission
rates go stale. Either state them with the month and year checked ("as listed on the
pricing page in March"), or describe the shape ("billed per seat, with a free tier")
and link to the source. Never copy them from another article.

## Information gain

Before drafting, write one sentence: "This article adds ___ that the top results do
not." Valid answers: the author's own measurement, a worked example from a real
project (with the author's input), a method for choosing, a clear verdict with
reasons, a comparison along criteria the others skip. "A better summary" is not one.
